\begin{abstract}
Unsupervised clustering is often proposed as a data-driven way to segment claims before reserving. We test that proposal on the simulated SPLICE portfolios: the full-complexity scenario in its standard and covariate versions, and then all five SPLICE scenarios.

An ex-post clustering of completed claims finds one natural split, between small, quickly settled claims and the rest. K-means recovers it far more stably than Ward's hierarchical method (bootstrap adjusted Rand index 0.96 against 0.49). Finer partitions depend mainly on how correlated measures of claim complexity are weighted.

We then use only information available at the valuation date and backtest against the actual run-off. Segmenting a paid chain ladder with clusters never beats random segmentations of the same sizes, in any of the five scenarios. Adjusting the case estimates of open claims with factors fitted out of time is the most reliable approach: it cuts the RBNS error from about 40\% to 7--12\% with a single factor, and groups add little to it.

Groups do help in three narrower roles:
\begin{itemize}
\item identifying large claims to reserve separately, which is the best approach under an inflation shock;
\item refining a model that accounts for calendar-period trends;
\item monitoring whether the claim mix or the development speed is changing.
\end{itemize}

The analysis also shows how inflation drift and the timing of information can create apparent gains. We propose five evaluation criteria and a random-segmentation placebo for any clustering used in reserving.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{intro}

Loss reserving is, at its core, an exercise in managing heterogeneity. Claims differ in size, in the delay before they are reported, in the time they take to settle, and in the pattern of payments and case-estimate revisions they generate. Traditional methods deal with this by aggregating claims into run-off triangles, segmented in advance by line of business, type of cover or accident period, and by assuming that development is homogeneous within each segment \parencite{taylor2000loss,england2002stochastic}. The segments are chosen by judgement, and the plausibility of the homogeneity assumption depends on how well that judgement was made.

Recent research has turned to individual-claim data and machine-learning methods \parencite{wuthrich2018machine}, supported by simulators that generate claim histories whose properties are fully known \parencite{avanzi2021synthetic,avanzi2023splice}. Within this landscape, unsupervised clustering is an appealing tool. Rather than imposing segments, it lets the data suggest groups of claims, or of portfolio cohorts, with similar development characteristics. Such groups could support segmentation \emph{before} reserving, or help to interpret the results of a reserving analysis. This is the question we investigate: can clustering claims by their development characteristics yield segments that are useful to a reserving actuary?

Clustering is, however, unforgiving in a specific way. It has no target variable, so there is no held-out error to tell us whether the result is right. K-means, hierarchical and model-based algorithms will return a partition of any data set, and that partition depends on decisions taken before the algorithm is run: what counts as an object (a claim or an occurrence period), which variables describe it, on what scale, under which distance and into how many groups \parencite{jain2010data,hastie2009elements}. Each of those decisions can be defended only with knowledge of the data. An analyst who skips the data-understanding stage does not avoid making them; the analyst makes them implicitly, and without evidence.

This paper therefore treats understanding the data as the first stage of the clustering study, not as a preliminary to it. Using the SPLICE Complexity~5 portfolio \parencite{avanzi2023splice}, we validate the structure of the data, examine the distribution of the candidate features and the dependence between them, and choose the unit of analysis. Our aim is twofold: to report the features of the data that matter for reserving, and to show that each of them translates into a concrete modelling decision, from the transformation of variables to the choice of algorithm. We then use those decisions to test how clustering can be used in reserving: to segment a chain-ladder triangle, to adjust the case estimates of open claims, to set large claims apart, to refine a model with calendar-period trends, and to monitor the portfolio. All tests use only information available at the valuation date, are scored against the actual run-off, and are repeated on all five SPLICE scenarios.

Section~\ref{methodology} describes the clustering and reserving experiments, and Section~\ref{results} reports their results. Section~\ref{conclusion} discusses the implications and limitations.

\section{Covariate data and reproducibility}
\label{app:cov}

The SPLICE repository publishes a covariate-adjusted version of each scenario, in which claim sizes depend on legal representation, injury severity and claimant age, but it does not publish the covariate assigned to each claim. The SynthETIC package distributes a covariate data set, \texttt{test\_covariates\_dataset}, but it labels SynthETIC's own example portfolio of 3,624 claims, whose per-period claim counts it matches exactly. It cannot be joined to the SPLICE Complexity~5 portfolio of 3,663 claims.

The SPLICE generator returns the covariate labels alongside the claims, so we regenerated the covariate portfolio with the published script (random seed 42) and exported the labels (repository script \texttt{scripts/export\_splice\_cov\_labels.R}). We found the following, using R~4.3.3:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{With the released versions} (SynthETIC 1.1.1 and SPLICE 1.1.2 from CRAN), the regenerated claim, payment and incurred tables match the published files to machine precision. This holds also when the generator is called twice in the same session. The labels we export therefore belong to the published files, and all covariate results in this paper use the published files with these labels.
\item \emph{With the development version of SynthETIC} (1.1.2 on GitHub, which changes how the random-number state is handled in the covariate step), the covariate labels and adjusted claim sizes are identical. Notification and settlement delays, payments and case-estimate histories, however, differ from the published files. An earlier draft of this work used that version by mistake.
\item \emph{For the same seed, the covariate and the standard portfolios differ} in everything drawn after the covariate step. This is expected, and the two should not be mixed.
\end{itemize}
Simulated data sets are thus reproducible for a given set of package versions, but not across them. We recommend publishing the data with the research, or documenting the versions used.
